The practical layer nobody mentions

Most botanical gardens, state parks, national parks and private estates require a photography permit if you bring a photographer, and several charge a proposal or event fee. Call two to four weeks ahead and ask specifically about "a proposal with a photographer" — staff usually have a standard answer and sometimes a preferred spot.

Carrying a ring on a plane

The ring goes in your carry-on, never in checked luggage — a checked bag is not covered for jewelry by any airline, and most travel insurance excludes it too. Keep it in a flat case in a bag that stays with you.

You do not have to take it out at security in most airports, and you do not have to open the box. If screening asks, a folded jacket over the bag is not the move — just hand it over quietly and ask for a private screening if your partner is standing right there. Crossing a border with a ring above your destination country's duty-free allowance can technically require a customs declaration; for a typical engagement ring worn or carried as a personal effect this rarely comes up, but it is worth a search on your specific route if the ring is expensive.

Insure it before you fly, with a policy that covers travel and loss rather than theft alone.

04 — Who to hire

The people who make it look effortless

We don't keep a directory. Listings go stale in every city at once, and a paid listing is an advert wearing a recommendation's clothes. What follows is what each of these people costs, what to ask them, and a search that finds them near you.

Proposal photographer

Most US sessions run $400–1,500, and nearly half of couples who hire one budget over $1,000. Price does not track session length the way you would expect: a sought-after specialist in an expensive city can charge more for fifteen minutes than a national booking service charges for a full hour. Judge the photographer, not the clock. This is the one worth paying for, because it is the only thing you cannot go back and get.

Ask: Have you shot at this spot before? Does it need a permit, and will you handle it? Where will you stand and which way should I face? What will you wear so you read as a tourist? How soon do I get two or three images I can send my family that night? What happens if we're late, or it rains?

Book this first6+ weeks in season

Picnic and setup company

The "luxury picnic" business exists in most cities now — rugs, low tables, cushions, lanterns, flowers. Roughly $250–800 depending on scale and how far they travel.

Ask: How long does setup take and when do you arrive? Do you need vehicle access to the spot? Who stays on site, and where will they wait? What's the rain policy and how late can I move it? Are you handling the park permit or am I? Who packs it down, and when?

Flowers and installation

An arch, a petal path, a wall of stems. Anywhere from $200 for something small to well over $1,500 installed. Beautiful in photographs, and the first thing wind ruins.

A solo guitarist, violinist or cellist, typically $200–600 for an hour. Extraordinary in the right setting and deeply awkward in the wrong one — a string player in an empty park announces that something is happening.

A drone operator

Spectacular footage, and the category with actual legal exposure. In the US commercial drone work requires an FAA Part 107 certificate, US national parks ban drones outright, and many city and state parks do too.

Ask: Can you send me your Part 107 certificate? Who is checking the airspace for this location and date? Is this park one that permits drones — in writing, not "it should be fine"?

Dinner at home cooked by someone else, or a table in the kitchen at a restaurant. Prices vary enormously by city and menu, so get a quote rather than a guess.

Four things that apply to all of them

Book the photographer first. Everyone else can usually be found at two weeks' notice. Good proposal photographers cannot, and between Thanksgiving and Valentine's Day they are gone months ahead.

Ask who is handling the permit, and get the answer in writing. Vendors will often say it's fine. "Fine" is not a permit, and it is you who gets moved on.

Pay deposits by credit card, not bank transfer. A card gives you a route to a refund if someone vanishes. This is a category with a lot of one-person businesses and a few that are not businesses at all.

Only the photography range is sourced. It comes from 2026 market pricing. The other figures are rough orientation from operator quotes and move a great deal by city — use them to judge whether a quote is sane, not as what you should pay, and always get three.

Cut — the only C you can see across a room

Cut is not the shape. It's how precisely the facets were placed: the angles, the proportions, the polish and the symmetry. A well-cut stone bounces light back up through the top. A poorly cut one leaks it out of the bottom and looks dull no matter how flawless the grading report says it is. If you cut one corner, do not cut this one.

BestWorst
What to ask
For a round brilliant, ask for GIA "Excellent" or AGS "Ideal" cut, with Excellent polish and symmetry. Fancy shapes get no official cut grade at all — judge those by proportions and by looking at them.
The numbers
A well-proportioned round sits near a 54–57% table, 59–62.5% total depth, a 34–35° crown angle and a 40.6–41° pavilion angle. Depth much over 63% usually means weight hidden underneath where you can't see it.
Three effects
Brilliance is white light return. Fire is the rainbow flash. Scintillation is the sparkle as it moves. Cut controls all three; nothing else does.

Color — graded on absence, not presence

The scale runs D to Z, measuring how little yellow or brown tint the stone has. Nobody starts at A — the scale deliberately began at D in 1953 to break from the vague "A, AA, AAA" grading that came before it. Stones are graded face-down against master stones under controlled light, which is nothing like how you'll ever see the ring.

D — ColorlessZ — Light yellow
Metal changes it
Yellow and rose gold hide warmth — an I, J or even K looks white set in gold and saves a great deal. Platinum and white gold are unforgiving: G or H is the sweet spot there.
Size changes it
Color is far more visible in larger stones. Under about 0.70 ct you can go two grades lower than you'd dare at 2 ct.
Shape changes it
Step cuts and elongated shapes concentrate color in the corners and tips. Go one grade higher for those than you would for a round.
Fluorescence
Strong blue fluorescence can make a warmer stone look whiter, and those stones often cost less. In a small minority it causes a milky haze — look at it in daylight rather than ruling it out on paper.

Clarity — where most people overspend

Clarity grades the internal inclusions and surface blemishes, viewed at 10× magnification by a trained grader. That last part is the whole point: a VS2 and a VVS1 look identical to the naked eye, and your partner is not going to carry a loupe. The word you want is not a grade at all — it's eye-clean.

FlawlessIncluded
The rule
VS2 is almost always eye-clean. SI1 often is, and is where the value lives — but you must look at that specific stone, or its plotted diagram, because it depends entirely on where the inclusion sits.
Position beats grade
An inclusion under the table in the middle is the worst place. One near the girdle edge can be hidden by a prong. Ask the jeweler to point to the plot on the report and show you.
Step cuts are strict
Emerald and asscher cuts have big open facets — a hall of mirrors with nowhere to hide. For those, VS2 or better, no exceptions.
Not all flaws
Inclusions are a stone's fingerprint and how a gemologist proves it's yours. A visible feather near the surface is a durability question worth asking about; a tiny crystal in the middle is not.

Carat — weight, not size

One carat is 0.2 grams, divided into 100 points. It measures mass, so two 1-carat stones can look noticeably different sizes depending on how they're cut and how much weight is hidden in the depth. Drag the slider — the stone is drawn to true millimeter scale.

1.00 ct 6.4 mm across
Magic sizes
Prices jump at the round numbers — 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.50, 2.00. A 0.92 ct is visually indistinguishable from a 1.00 ct and can cost noticeably less. Ask for stones just under a threshold.
Spread
Elongated shapes cover more finger per carat than a round. An oval typically reads about a quarter to a half carat larger than it weighs.
Finger size matters
The same stone looks materially bigger on a size 4 hand than a size 8. Judge proportion on their hand, not in a photograph of someone else's.
Halos
A halo of small stones adds 0.3–0.5 ct of apparent size for a fraction of the cost. It also dates faster than a plain solitaire — a real trade-off, not a trick.

Shape — the decision they'll actually notice

Cut quality is invisible until you compare. Shape is the thing your partner already has an opinion about. Tap one to set it on the spec sheet.

Lab-grown vs natural

Chemically identical, commercially not

A lab-grown diamond is a diamond — same carbon lattice, same hardness, same sparkle, and only distinguishable by specialist equipment. It's grown by CVD or HPHT in weeks instead of billions of years. It now accounts for 61% of engagement ring purchases, and at the same budget it buys roughly twice the stone.

The honest trade-off: lab-grown prices have fallen steeply and continue to, so resale value is close to nothing. Natural stones hold value better and carry a rarity story some people care about and others actively don't. Neither answer is wrong — but decide which of those two things you're buying before you walk in.

Either way, insist on a report from GIA or AGS (strictest) or IGI (the norm for lab-grown), and check that the report number is laser-inscribed on the girdle. Be wary of EGL grades, which run soft by a grade or two.

06 — The ring

Setting, metal, size, budget

The stone gets all the attention and the setting does all the work. This is where you match the ring to their actual hands and actual week.

Solitaire

One stone, four or six prongs. The default for a reason: it never looks dated and it puts every dollar into the center. Six prongs are more secure; four show more stone.

ClassicResizes easily

Bezel

A metal rim around the whole stone. The most secure setting there is, and the one that doesn't snag on gloves, hair, climbing holds or patients. Slightly modern, slightly less sparkle.

Best for active hands

Halo

A ring of small stones around the center, making it read half a carat larger. Maximum apparent size per dollar. A hidden halo — visible only from the side — gets the sparkle without the era stamp.

Maximizes size

Three-stone

A center with two side stones — often given the past/present/future reading. Adds width and presence, and lets a smaller center carry a bigger ring.

Wide profile

Pavé & micropavé

Tiny stones set along the band. Enormous sparkle, and the small stones do occasionally come loose over the years. Full-coverage and eternity bands are very hard to resize later.

Resizing limited

Vintage & milgrain

Beaded edges, engraving, filigree. Genuine antique rings often hold old mine or old European cuts, which sparkle differently — broader, warmer flashes made for candlelight rather than LEDs.

Character

If none of those are the ring

Plenty of people don't want a solitaire, and a few don't want a diamond or a ring at all. These are the real alternatives, with the real trade-offs — the biggest of which is almost always resizing.

Toi et moi

Two stones side by side — "you and me" — often two different shapes or two different stones entirely. Georgian in origin, thoroughly back. Reads as a story rather than a status object.

Very hard to resize

East-west

An elongated stone turned sideways across the finger instead of along it. One rotation, and a familiar shape stops looking familiar. Costs nothing extra and suits an oval, emerald, marquise or kite.

Free way to be different

Cluster or constellation

Many small stones arranged as a shape — a flower, a scatter, a deliberate asymmetry. Antique in lineage, and it buys visual size for far less than one large stone.

More presence per dollarMore to catch

No center stone

A carved, engraved, hammered or wide band with nothing raised on it at all. For someone who genuinely doesn't want a stone, this is the answer — and it's the single most durable thing you can put on a hand.

IndestructibleLowest cost

An heirloom reset

Their grandmother's stone in a setting built for them. Costs a fraction of a new ring, carries meaning nothing else can buy, and the old cut will look different from anything modern — which is the point.

Have it checked first

Not a ring

A necklace, a watch, a tattoo appointment booked for the following week, a tree planted in the yard. Right for someone whose work or hands make a ring impractical, or who simply doesn't wear rings.

Stones that aren't diamonds

What people actually choose

Sapphire is the serious answer — Mohs 9, and it comes in teal, peach, parti (two colors in one stone), grey, green and colorless, not only blue. Montana sapphires have traceable US origin, which solves the sourcing question at the same time.

Salt-and-pepper diamonds are real diamonds full of visible black inclusions — the flaws are the design. Cheap, unrepeatable, and every one is different. Spinel (Mohs 8) is vivid, natural and almost never treated. Moissanite throws more rainbow fire than diamond, which people either love or immediately clock.

Be careful with opal, moonstone, moss agate and raw or rough stones. They photograph beautifully and they are soft — expect visible wear within a couple of years on a ring worn daily. That can be an acceptable trade if you both know it going in.

The three traps

What goes wrong with unusual rings

  • Resizing. Toi et moi, east-west, clusters, eternity bands and anything with stones all the way around can often be moved half a size at most, sometimes not at all. Get the measurement right the first time or expect to remake the band.
  • No grading language. Colored stones aren't graded on the D–Z or VS scales, so there is no report to hide behind and no standard to compare against. You are buying with your eyes and on the seller's reputation. Ask who cut it and where it came from.
  • Replacement. A one-of-a-kind stone can't be replaced with an identical one if it's lost. Insure it with an appraisal that describes it specifically, and photograph it from several angles before it goes on a hand.

Getting the size

If they don't know you're asking

  • Borrow a ring they already wear on that finger — the ring finger or the one next to it — and take it to a jeweler. Don't guess from a different finger.
  • Press that ring into a bar of soap or plasticine for a clean impression if you can't leave the house with it.
  • Recruit their most discreet friend or sibling to go "ring shopping for themselves" and try things on together.
  • Accept a near miss. The average US size is around 6 to 6.5, and resizing a plain band is routine.

If you're doing this together

Plenty of couples are — they picked the ring, or you're both getting one, or the whole thing was decided over dinner months ago and only the moment is still a secret. Then don't do any of the above. Go and get properly sized at a jeweler, on a normal weekday, in the afternoon.

It takes ten minutes, it is free, it removes the single most common reason a ring has to go back, and it is the one part of this that is genuinely better when nobody is being clever about it. If you're both wearing something, get both fingers measured in the same visit and order the pair together so the metals match.

Fingers change size. They swell in heat, after salt, at the end of the day and on flights. Measure at normal room temperature, and remember a wide band fits tighter than a thin one — go up about a quarter size for anything over 4mm.

Insure it before you propose

The window where a ring is most likely to be lost is the week you're carrying it around in a coat pocket waiting for the right moment. Get a valuation from the jeweler and add a scheduled personal property rider to your renter's or homeowner's policy, or a standalone jewelry policy — typically 1–2% of the ring's value per year. Photograph the report number on the girdle while you have it.

07 — How

Six ways to actually do it

The method matters more than the venue. These run from most private to most public — find where your partner sits on that line and don't drift past it to impress anyone else. How it gets remembered is a separate decision, and it is further down this page.

01

The ordinary Tuesday

No occasion, no reservation, no warning. Making dinner, out with the animal, on the sofa. The whole meaning is that it wasn't a production — that this is what the rest of it looks like. Impossible to see coming, and the version most private people describe afterwards as perfect.

Most privateNo cost
02

The walk that ends somewhere

A hike, a beach, a route you've done a hundred times, with one spot chosen in advance. The walking gives you both something to do with the nerves, and arriving somewhere makes a natural moment to stop. Scout it in person at the same hour, on a similar day.

PrivateWeather plan needed
03

Dinner, then the real reason

Propose before or after the meal, never during. Before, and you both get to enjoy dinner engaged; after, and they don't spend two courses suspicious. Doing it at the table in a full restaurant turns strangers into an audience — only choose that deliberately.

Semi-publicCall the venue ahead
04

The staged errand

A believable reason to be somewhere and dressed well — a friend's fake party, a work thing, a portrait session "for your parents". The cover story does the heavy lifting: it explains the outfit, the location, and why you're nervous.

Needs accomplicesHigh surprise
05

The long game

A letter a week, a photo album finished on the last page, a scavenger hunt through the places that got you here. Takes weeks of preparation and lands hardest with sentimental people who keep things. Make sure the final step is somewhere you can be alone.

High effortHigh meaning
06

The reveal, then the room

Propose just the two of you, then walk into a bar or a living room where everyone they love is already waiting. The best of both — a private yes, followed immediately by the celebration. Give them a moment in the car first.

The one that is not on that list

Choosing the ring together. This gets written up as a way of proposing, and it isn't one — it answers a different question. You can choose the ring together and still ask on a trailhead, over dinner, or in a room full of people; all six above are still open to you. What it removes is the single risk you cannot undo, which is buying something they will wear every day and quietly not like. Increasingly common, and the right answer whenever they have opinions about jewelry. The only secret left is when, and that is plenty.

How it gets remembered

A separate decision from the one above, and one most people reduce to "do we hire a photographer or not". There are six answers, and hiring somebody is only one of them — for a lot of proposals it is the wrong one. The order below runs from the most arranged to the least.

01

A photographer, at a distance

Waiting with a long lens and looking like a tourist. You get their real face in the real second — the one photograph people actually keep — and a short set of portraits once they know. Sessions typically run 30–60 minutes. Agree a signal, a direction to face, and a plan for running late.

The real face, keptBook 6+ weeks out
02

The person who brings them

Whoever is getting them to the spot is already in on it and already going to be there — the sibling, the friend with the excuse about brunch. Give them one job for thirty seconds: stand where you tell them, press record before you start, and keep filming past the part where everyone starts crying. Brief them on the phone, not by text.

Already thereFreePractise the angle
03

Somebody who works there

The server, the guide, the person on the desk at the conservatory. They are present, it costs nothing, and at a venue that sees proposals they have almost certainly done this before and will have a view on where you should stand. Ask when you call about the booking, not on the day — and get a name.

No lead timeAsk when you book
04

A phone, propped and already running

On a shelf, a wall, a rock, a car roof. Start it early and let it run — storage is cheaper than the moment. It is the only practical way to photograph a proposal at home, and it produces the frame nobody is performing for. Leave it going afterwards: the call they make two minutes later is often the better footage.

Works at homeCheck the frame first
05

Portraits straight afterwards, not during

Nobody captures the moment itself; you take the pictures ten minutes later, with the ring on and the adrenaline still going. Far easier to arrange, much cheaper, and the pictures are of two people who are visibly delighted rather than two people being observed. Get one of just their hand — that is the photograph they will send to everybody.

Easiest to arrangePick the spot ahead
06

Nothing at all

No camera, no accomplice, no phone. You keep the only copy, which is the point — a surprising number of people say afterwards that not having it on film is what made it theirs. Tell one person the same evening if you want it witnessed. It is the one choice here that a few people do regret, and the propped phone costs you nothing if you are unsure.

The hour before
  • Don't put the box in your trouser pocket — it prints through. Use a flat travel case in a coat, or give the box to an accomplice.
  • Take the ring out of the box beforehand if you're nervous about fumbling. Several people have dropped it opening the lid.
  • Charge your phone. Someone will need to take the first photo and you'll want to call people afterwards.
  • Know the first two sentences you want to say. Not a speech — you will not remember a speech.
  • Decide in advance who they get to call first, and leave time for it. That call matters more than the dinner reservation.
Two questions worth settling early

Do they want family asked?

The modern framing is asking whether your partner would want you to, rather than asking anyone for permission. For some families it means a great deal; for others it lands badly. This is knowable in advance — it's come up in conversation before, you just have to remember what they said.

Who knows in advance?

Every person you tell is a person who can accidentally tell. Keep the circle to the people who are genuinely needed — the photographer, the sibling who got the ring size, the friend hosting the room afterwards — and tell them the date, not the details.

08 — What to say

You will not remember a speech

Every other part of this is planning. This part is thirty seconds long, happens once, and is the only bit you cannot outsource. The good news is that the bar is far lower than you think, and almost everything that goes wrong here goes wrong the same way.

The shape that works

Three sentences, in this order

  • One specific thing about them. Not "you're amazing" — the actual thing. The way they call their sister every time they drive home. What they did the week your dad was in hospital. Specific is what makes someone cry; general is what makes them wait politely for you to finish.
  • What you want. Plainly, in your own words. "I want to do the rest of it with you" beats anything you could borrow from a film.
  • The question, out loud. "Will you marry me?" Said, not implied.

That's it. Under thirty seconds. You are not performing — you are asking someone something, and they are extremely on your side.

The mistake nearly everyone makes

They never actually ask

It happens constantly. Someone delivers the whole thing, holds out the ring, and then trails off — and the other person is left standing there working out whether a question has occurred. Sometimes minutes pass. Sometimes they have to ask "are you asking me?", which is not the memory anyone wanted.

Say the words. Whatever else you forget, the last four are the ones that matter.

Say it out loud

The other five ways it goes sideways

01

The toast

Listing their achievements as though you're at the wedding already. It sounds like an introduction, not a proposal, and it puts them in the audience rather than in the moment with you.

02

The apology

"I know this isn't much, and I know I should have…" Nerves make people undersell. Nobody has ever wanted their proposal to open with a disclaimer.

03

The essay

Four minutes of material while they stand there holding their own face. Long does not read as sincere; it reads as rehearsed, and it gives them nothing to do. Anything you cut, you can say over dinner afterwards.

04

The riddle

An elaborate setup they have to decode. Scavenger hunts and clues are wonderful as the journey — but the arrival should be unmistakable. Never make someone guess whether this is the thing.

05

The pressure

"Everyone's here, so…" — said in a room of people, with a phone up. Even as a joke it converts a question into an obligation. If a sentence would land differently in private, don't use it in public.

If it goes wrong in your head

Have one sentence memorized, and only one

Memorize the last line. Nothing else. If you blank on everything in the middle, you can still land it — and people who blank and then simply say "I had a whole thing, but: will you marry me?" tend to get the better story out of it.

If you cry, keep going. Nobody in the history of this has minded. It is not a performance with marks for composure.

Then stop talking. The ten seconds after the question belong to them. Don't fill the silence, don't explain, don't add a footnote about the ring. Let them answer.

Practice, once

Out loud, in the car

In your head does not count. Sentences that are fine in your head fall apart in a mouth, and you want to find that out on a Tuesday rather than at the top of a hill. Say it once, alone, at normal speaking volume.

Once is enough. Rehearse it twenty times and you'll deliver it like a reading.

On the knee

Entirely optional, and nobody minds either way. If you're going to: decide beforehand which knee, and look at the ground first. Wet grass, gravel and sand are all worse than they look, and the deciding factor is usually what you're wearing.

Three examples, in different registers

Plain. "I think about you when nothing's happening. Just doing the dishes, driving to work — you're the thing my head goes to. I want that for the rest of my life. Will you marry me?"

Specific. "You drove four hours to sit in a hospital corridor with me and you never once made it about you. I knew then. I've known since. Will you marry me?"

Light. "I had a speech. It was very good. I've forgotten all of it except the important part — will you marry me?"

09 — When

Pick a date, work backwards

Most of what goes wrong is a lead time somebody didn't know about. Enter your target date and we'll lay out what has to happen and by when.

    Choosing the date itself

    What the calendar does to you

    Engagement season runs from Thanksgiving through Valentine's Day and takes a large share of the year's proposals. Everyone is together, everyone is off work, and the photos look like the holidays. The cost: photographers book out, restaurants are full, and their family may half-expect it.

    The off-season — March through October — buys you availability, surprise, better light, and venues that aren't crowded. If nothing is forcing December, this is the easier path.

    Golden hour is the hour after sunrise and before sunset. Look up sunset for your exact date, then arrive 90 minutes before — many spots fall into shadow well before the sun is technically down.

    Avoid borrowing a date. Proposing on their birthday or a family anniversary means that day now belongs to two things forever. Some people love that. Ask yourself which kind they are.

    Weekdays are underrated. Quieter venues, empty overlooks, and a Tuesday that becomes memorable on its own.

    If the date is close

    Ready-to-ship settings with a stone already on hand can be done in one to two weeks. If you're tighter than that, propose with a temporary band, an heirloom, or the empty box and design the real ring together afterwards — which many people prefer anyway. Rushing a custom CAD order is how rings arrive wrong.

    Privacy

    What happens to what you type here

    You are describing someone you love, and planning a surprise. Both are reasons to know exactly where the words go. The short version: what you type stays in your browser unless you press a button that sends it, and there are exactly two such buttons — Send it on the plan email, and Build the trip when it asks for destination ideas. Nothing you write about your partner goes through either one.

    The quiz

    Stays on your device

    Every answer you give — the multiple choice, the boxes you tick about what your partner has actually said, and the free text where you describe them in your own words — is read by code running inside your own browser. It is matched against a keyword list that is part of this page. None of it is sent to us, and none of it is written to your device. Close the tab and it is gone.

    Changing the recommendation afterwards — swapping the place, the method or the ring — also happens entirely in your browser. We do not learn that you changed it, or what you changed it to. That is a real cost to us: it is the single most useful thing we could know about whether any of this works, and we would rather have the promise than the data.

    The same is true of the diamond bench, the date timeline, the budget figures and the outreach notes. This site has no database. There is no account to make, and nothing to log in to.

    The plan link

    Carries scores and ticks, not sentences

    The link you can copy at the end of the quiz holds four things: the numeric scores your answers added up to, the boxes you ticked on the last step, anything you changed by hand afterwards, and the city you typed, if you typed one.

    The ticks and the changes travel as short fixed labels from a list built into this page — nopublic, sizeknown, stone.sapphire — the same kind of thing as a multiple-choice answer. Some of them — a stone they named, that they do not want a ring, that a particular person should be there — say something the scores alone do not, which is the point of asking. What the link never holds is a sentence you wrote. Neither the free text on any question nor the box at the end of the last step goes into the link, which is why the quotes you see in the result do not come back when you reopen it. That is deliberate, and it is the reason it is safe to send this link to yourself.

    That data sits after the # in the address. Browsers do not send the part after a # to the server, so when you copy the link or paste it somewhere, we do not see it and neither does anyone hosting it. The link decodes in whichever browser opens it, and anything in it that this page does not recognise is thrown away rather than shown to you.

    Leaving this site

    Where the links take you

    Several links here are built out of what you typed, and clicking one hands that much to wherever it lands. Typed text reaches exactly three companies this way: Google, Booking.com and Airbnb. A fourth, Anthropic, receives the trip brief where destination ideas are switched on — see the card below, which also tells you whether they are switched on for you. Under Trip, the flight, hotel and stay links carry your city and your dates, and the sunset and weather searches carry the place and the date as well. Under Locations and Who to hire, the map searches carry the place you named. Nothing goes anywhere until you click, and you can always run the same search yourself.

    Once you follow any link out — to those sites, to a jeweler, to a photographer — you are on their site and their terms apply, not ours. We have no relationship with any of them and are paid nothing for sending you.

    The first thing that leaves

    Emailing yourself the plan

    If you use the email button, two things travel to our server and on to Resend, the service that delivers the message: the address you typed and the plan link — plus, if you ticked it, the fact that you ticked the box. Nothing else, and no part of the quiz beyond what the link already holds. We do not keep a copy of any of it.

    Resend keeps delivery records — that a message to that address was sent, and whether it arrived — the way every email service does. Their privacy terms are at resend.com/legal/privacy-policy.

    If you tick the box asking to hear from us, your address is added to a mailing list and stays there until you unsubscribe, which every message we send will let you do in one click. Leave it unticked and your address is used once, to send you that one email, and then not again.

    The second thing that leaves

    Asking for destination ideas

    The Trip section can ask a language model — Claude, run by Anthropic — for three destinations that fit what you described. Where that is switched on, pressing Build the trip sends the brief you typed: the thing you want to do, the destination if you named one, where you are leaving from, your dates and your budget.     Where it is not switched on, the button builds the flight, hotel and timing searches and sends nothing anywhere.     The flight, hotel and map searches themselves are a different thing: they are ordinary links, and nothing reaches Google, Booking.com or Airbnb until you click one.

    Everything else

    The unavoidable bits

    No cookies, no analytics. This site sets no cookies and runs no analytics or advertising scripts. We cannot tell how many people take the quiz or what they answer, which is a real cost to us and the right trade.

    Server logs. The site is hosted on Cloudflare, which records ordinary request information — IP address, time, which page, which browser — to deliver pages and block attacks. That happens for every website you visit and we do not add anything to it.

    Typefaces. The three fonts are served by Google Fonts, so loading any page here tells Google your IP address, as it does on a large share of the web. We would rather it didn't; serving the files ourselves is on the list.

    Stopping abuse of the email button. An open send button on a public site gets used to send mail nobody asked for, so we count how many plan emails each internet connection has asked for in the past day. The count holds times only — no email addresses, no plans — and nothing older than twenty-four hours is kept.

    The practical warning

    None of the above protects a surprise from a shared inbox. If you email yourself the plan, or leave the link in a browser you both use, the person it is about can find it. Use an address only you open, and delete the message once you have what you need. That is the likeliest way this goes wrong — not us.
