08 — What to say

You will not remember a speech

Every other part of this is planning. This part is thirty seconds long, happens once, and is the only bit you cannot outsource. The good news is that the bar is far lower than you think, and almost everything that goes wrong here goes wrong the same way.

The shape that works

Three sentences, in this order

  1. 1
    One specific thing about them.

    Not "you're amazing" — the actual thing. The way they call their sister every time they drive home. What they did the week your dad was in hospital. Specific is what makes someone cry; general is what makes them wait politely for you to finish.

  2. 2
    What you want.

    Plainly, in your own words. "I want to do the rest of it with you" beats anything you could borrow from a film.

  3. 3
    The question, out loud.

    "Will you marry me?" Said, not implied.

That's it. Under thirty seconds. You are not performing — you are asking someone something, and they are extremely on your side.

The mistake nearly everyone makes

They never actually ask

It happens constantly. Someone delivers the whole thing, holds out the ring, and then trails off — and the other person is left standing there working out whether a question has occurred. Sometimes minutes pass. Sometimes they have to ask "are you asking me?", which is not the memory anyone wanted.

Say the words. Whatever else you forget, the last four are the ones that matter.

Say it out loud

The other five ways it goes sideways

01

The toast

Listing their achievements as though you're at the wedding already. It sounds like an introduction, not a proposal, and it puts them in the audience rather than in the moment with you.

02

The apology

"I know this isn't much, and I know I should have…" Nerves make people undersell. Nobody has ever wanted their proposal to open with a disclaimer.

03

The essay

Four minutes of material while they stand there holding their own face. Long does not read as sincere; it reads as rehearsed, and it gives them nothing to do. Anything you cut, you can say over dinner afterwards.

04

The riddle

An elaborate setup they have to decode. Scavenger hunts and clues are wonderful as the journey — but the arrival should be unmistakable. Never make someone guess whether this is the thing.

05

The pressure

"Everyone's here, so…" — said in a room of people, with a phone up. Even as a joke it converts a question into an obligation. If a sentence would land differently in private, don't use it in public.

If it goes wrong in your head

Have one sentence memorized, and only one

Memorize the last line. Nothing else. If you blank on everything in the middle, you can still land it — and people who blank and then simply say "I had a whole thing, but: will you marry me?" tend to get the better story out of it.

If you cry, keep going. Nobody in the history of this has minded. It is not a performance with marks for composure.

Then stop talking. The ten seconds after the question belong to them. Don't fill the silence, don't explain, don't add a footnote about the ring. Let them answer.

Practice, once

Out loud, in the car

In your head does not count. Sentences that are fine in your head fall apart in a mouth, and you want to find that out on a Tuesday rather than at the top of a hill. Say it once, alone, at normal speaking volume.

Once is enough. Rehearse it twenty times and you'll deliver it like a reading.

On the knee

Entirely optional, and nobody minds either way. If you're going to: decide beforehand which knee, and look at the ground first. Wet grass, gravel and sand are all worse than they look, and the deciding factor is usually what you're wearing.
